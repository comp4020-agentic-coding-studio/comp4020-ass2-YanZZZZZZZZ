No new subsystem — this session is the crit itself, held to the same
legible-failure standard as everything it studies. The full argument for
why that standard applies to feedback as much as to level design is in
[The bonfire crit](/topics/the-bonfire-crit/).

## The argument

"Your map didn't feel connected" is the arbitrary case: a mood standing in
for information, the same failure Week 1 named in a damage-sponge boss.
"The elevator shortcut in your sketch reconnects to a room the player never
saw before riding it, so it reads as a second entrance rather than an
earned shortcut" is the punishing case, in the sense this course has used
that word all semester — it costs the presenter something to hear, and it
tells them exactly what to fix, the way Week 3's shortcut standard already
defined what "earned" means. A crit run on encouragement alone teaches
nothing a rejection would have; one run on demolition alone just says the
same nothing more loudly. Today's peer review is graded on whether it hits
the punishing case, not on tone.

This mirrors Week 6's midpoint review deliberately: the same room, largely
the same peers, checking a finished Final Project one-pager against the
same map, combat, boss and guidance standards they applied to a Boss
Blueprint eleven weeks earlier. What's different is the object under
review — four parts (map sketch, two item descriptions, a boss's move
summary, an opening guidance script) that have to argue the same thing
about one opening level, not four separate exercises graded in isolation.
A peer review that praises the item descriptions and the map sketch
separately hasn't actually checked whether the boss's transition makes
sense given what those descriptions already established.

## Outline

- feedback as either legible (names the specific decision) or arbitrary
  (a tone standing in for information) — the genre's own test, applied to
  critique
- a worked example of each: "didn't feel connected" versus a shortcut that
  fails Week 3's earned-connection standard, named specifically
- how today mirrors Week 6's midpoint review, applied to a finished project
  instead of a Boss Blueprint
- why the four parts of the Final Project have to be checked as one
  argument, not four separately-graded exercises
- presenting the Final Project one-pager and submitting the Death Notes
  portfolio
- peer review structured around the same map, combat, boss and guidance
  checks used all semester
- the closing address: what should outlast the scaffolding once it's gone

## Into the session

Bring a presentable draft of your Final Project one-pager and your
completed Death Notes portfolio, ready to submit. Each student presents
their opening level in a few minutes; peer review focuses on whether the
map, the item descriptions, the boss's transition and the opening guidance
script argue the same thing about the level, not on whether any one part
works in isolation. The session closes with the semester's address — not a
recap, but a statement of what should keep operating in your own judgment
once the weekly lectures and spec checks are gone.
