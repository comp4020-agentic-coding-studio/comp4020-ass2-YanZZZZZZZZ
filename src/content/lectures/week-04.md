"Hard" gets blamed on reaction speed; the actual test is whether an attack
was readable before it landed. The full argument for how telegraphs,
stamina and animation commitment combine into one grammar is in
[Combat legibility](/topics/combat-legibility/) — including the difference
between a telegraph a player can learn to beat and a genuinely unreadable
attack that only looks like the same design.

## The argument

The genre's own entries don't agree on tempo, which is what makes tempo the
wrong thing to blame difficulty on. Bloodborne rewards aggression outright
— the rally mechanic pays back health for hitting back fast after taking
damage, which is a faster, more forward game than Dark Souls' shield-and-
patience default. Sekiro is faster again, and removes the option to
disengage almost entirely: posture damage means standing and reading is
frequently the correct answer, not the cautious one. Three different
speeds, and the same underlying grammar in all three — a wind-up the game
commits to showing in full, a resource that has to be spent to act, and an
input that can't be recalled once thrown.

The failure case worth naming is the "phantom hitbox": an attack whose
visual effect reads as bigger or smaller than the volume that actually
deals damage, so a player who reads the animation correctly still gets hit,
or dodges an attack that was never going to connect. This is a different
and lesser design than a hard read — it's not the fight withholding
information, it's the fight lying about the information it just showed you.
Legibility only works as a design if there's no daylight between what the
animation promises and what the hitbox delivers; the moment those two
diverge, "watch and learn" stops being good advice.

## Outline

- reaction speed versus legibility, and why the genre's average attack
  speed argues for the second
- Bloodborne's rally versus Sekiro's posture: two different tempos, one
  grammar underneath both
- telegraph, stamina, animation commitment as three withholdings, not three
  separate systems
- poise as a hidden metronome: why trading hits is sometimes the correct
  read, not a failed one
- the phantom-hitbox failure: when the animation and the actual hit volume
  disagree, legibility breaks even if the wind-up was long
- this week's workshop: a frame-by-frame breakdown of three attacks, written
  up as an attack-tells cheatsheet

## Into the session

Bring footage or a replay of three boss attacks you've died to more than
once — ideally the boss you started building in Week 2. We'll step through
them frame by frame on the shared screen, marking exactly where each
telegraph resolves into an outcome a trained eye could call versus where an
untrained one is still guessing, then write the three up as a cheatsheet
you'll reuse when Week 5 asks you to design a transition your own attacks
have to survive.
