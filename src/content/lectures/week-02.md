Most games hide the checkpoint outside the fiction. Dark Souls makes it the
premise: you are cursed Undead, and death returns you to the world a little
more hollow than before. Every item description in this course is testimony
from someone already dead — the corpse writing back, not a narrator
reporting from outside the fiction. The full argument for what that
licenses — and why an unreliable narrator only works when the mechanic
motivates the unreliability — is in
[Death as a narrative verb](/topics/death-as-narrative-verb/).

## The argument

Most respawn systems solve the authorial problem by not solving it: a
shooter's checkpoint is a courtesy the fiction never acknowledges, a "you
were merely stunned" the game doesn't even bother stating aloud. Dark Souls
instead builds an entire cosmology around the fact that you keep coming
back — the Undead Curse isn't a wrapper around the mechanic, it's the
mechanic's only honest explanation, and every NPC who mentions hollowing is
talking about your save file without knowing it. Bloodborne runs a
different version of the same move: Insight doesn't bring you back to life,
it changes what you're sane enough to perceive, so an item description
written by a scholar deep in Insight reads as unreliable for a reason the
system itself supplies, not because the prose decided to sound cryptic.

That's the craft test this week's workshop enforces. An item description
that merely sounds strange is atmosphere borrowed from the genre; one whose
strangeness the mechanic explains — this ring's wearer had died enough
times to misremember their own name — is testimony. The failure case is
easy to spot once you're looking for it: a "flavour text" line bolted onto
a stat block, written in a voice no in-world event actually produced. If
you can delete the mechanical justification and the prose reads exactly
the same, the unreliability was decorative.

## Into the session

Bring a one-paragraph sketch of an original boss — what it was before it
became one, and what it specifically lost or misunderstood in becoming
one — and write three item descriptions belonging to it: a weapon, a piece
of armour, a trinket. Each has to be testimony from a narrator your world
has already made unreliable, not a stat block wearing a costume. The room
will try to reconstruct what actually happened to your boss from the three
descriptions alone; if they can do it too easily, you wrote plot summary.
