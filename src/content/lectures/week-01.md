Punishing and cheating difficulty can look identical on a death counter.
The test that tells them apart — full argument in
[Punishing difficulty vs. cheating difficulty](/topics/masochism-club/) — is
whether the player, the instant after dying, has a specific and legible
reason to believe the next attempt can win. Every later week studies one
mechanism a Souls-like uses to keep that reason legible: death's narrative
framing, a level's shape, an attack's grammar, a boss's staging.

## The argument

Sekiro's Lady Butterfly is this course's reference case for a reason: her
double-jump into a dive attack telegraphs for long enough to deflect or
dodge on the first honest attempt, and the lesson it teaches — watch the
altitude, not the sword — survives being learned once. Set that against the
pattern most "cheap" encounters share: an ambush sprung the instant a fog
gate closes, three weapons already swinging before the room has told the
player it exists. Both can produce an identical death counter. Only one
gives the player a specific decision to point at afterward.

The boundary isn't always this clean, and the genre's own patch history
shows the work of finding it. Elden Ring's Malenia shipped with a Waterfowl
Dance whose dodge window was, by most players' account, tighter than its
telegraph justified — punishing in intent, cheating in practice, until a
balance patch widened the window without touching the animation. The fix
didn't make the attack much easier to survive; it made it survivable by
reading it, which is the only axis this course grades on. The opposite
failure is more common and less interesting: a boss that is simply the
previous boss's health bar tripled, no new tell, no new pattern — the shape
this course calls the damage sponge, and the first thing every later
week's workshop is built to avoid producing by accident.

## Outline

- why "difficulty" undersells what the genre is actually doing
- the test: legible failure versus arbitrary failure, not damage numbers
- Lady Butterfly's dive versus a fog-gate ambush, side by side
- Malenia's Waterfowl Dance as a case the genre itself had to re-tune, not
  a settled example
- the damage-sponge failure mode: more health and more damage without a
  new tell
- why this test has to survive being applied to your own workshop
  artefacts, not just to games you already admire
- how this semester's workshops each produce an artefact, not just an
  essay about one

## Into the session

Today's session runs the test cold, before any later week supplies
vocabulary for it: play or watch one attempt-and-death cycle against a
widely agreed punishing case and one you'd call cheap, and log the exact
moment each one either earned back your trust or lost it. The log you build
today is the same instrument every later workshop hands you a new
subsystem to run it against.
