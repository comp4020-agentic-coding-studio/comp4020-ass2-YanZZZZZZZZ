A figure who never states their goal plainly is still giving directions,
just not the kind a quest log prints — and a genre built on withheld
comfort has little reason to hand back an uncomplicated happy ending at the
finish line either. Write the bad ending first, this week's workshop
insists, because a "true" ending that only ever compromises between good
and bad hasn't earned either. The full argument for both is in
[Weak guidance and the value of a "bad" ending](/topics/weak-guidance-and-bad-endings/).

## The argument

Sekiro sharpens Dark Souls' ambiguity rather than softening it. None of its
four endings — Immortal Severance, Purification, Return, Shura — is
uncomplicated good, and Shura in particular reads as close to a worst-case
outcome in tone (the protagonist consumed by the same battle-hunger the
game spent hours warning against) without the game ever labelling it that
way in-fiction. A player has to arrive at "this was the bad one" through
inference, the same read this week's workshop asks students to write
toward rather than announce. Dark Souls' kindled-versus-dark split works
the same way from a different angle: kindling a dying flame for one more
age of an already exhausted world, or letting it go out and something
genuinely unknown begin — neither framed by the game as the reward path.

The failure case is a "true" ending unlocked by a completion checklist
rather than earned by understanding: a structure where seeing everything
the game has to offer is what the true ending requires, which turns tonal
payoff into a scavenger-hunt reward instead of a shift the player's own
grasp of the fiction produces. A true ending earned by information — you
finally understand what the flame actually costs, who actually benefits
from kindling it — is a different, better kind of "true" than one earned by
percentage completed, and it's the version this course's own true endings
are graded against.

## Into the session

Bring the boss and world you've been building since Week 2 — or whatever
original setting you're using for the Final Project. In the session you'll
write three 50-word endings for that world in the same unreliable-testimony
voice as your Week 2 item descriptions, none announcing its own category.
If a reader can sort your three into good, bad and true without hesitation,
the ambiguity hasn't been earned yet.
