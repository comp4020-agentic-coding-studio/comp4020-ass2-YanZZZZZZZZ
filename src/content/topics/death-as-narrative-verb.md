---
title: Death as a narrative verb
description:
  How the Undead Curse and hollowing turn respawn from a mechanic that needs
  excusing into the plot itself, and what that licenses in item text
tags: [narrative, death]
related:
  - lectures/week-02
---

Every game with a checkpoint has the same authorial problem: the player
dies, and dies again, and the fiction has to explain why the world doesn't
notice. Most games solve it by not trying — a respawn is understood to sit
outside the story, a courtesy to the player rather than an event inside the
world. Dark Souls solves it by making the problem the premise. You are
cursed Undead; death does not expel you from the world, it returns you to
it, again, a little more hollow than before. The mechanic a shooter would
hide behind a loading screen becomes, here, the thing the entire setting is
about.

This licenses a specific kind of writing the genre leans on constantly: item
description as unreliable narration. A ring's flavour text doesn't explain
its stat bonus; it tells you what the previous owner believed about it, in
a voice already half-mad from having died too many times to trust their own
memory. The description of the Rite of Kindling, or a covenant's founding
myth, never confirms itself — it is testimony from someone Undead, which
this course's Week 1 test already tells you to distrust: a hollowed
narrator's account of events is exactly the kind of failure that might be
arbitrary rather than legible, and the game never resolves which.

The craft lesson underneath the lore is precise: write the description from
inside a character who has died enough times to be unreliable, and let the
mechanic (death, hollowing, respawn) motivate the unreliability rather than
excuse it. A description that merely sounds cryptic without a reason an
Undead narrator would sound that way is atmosphere borrowed from the genre,
not narrative built on its actual premise.
