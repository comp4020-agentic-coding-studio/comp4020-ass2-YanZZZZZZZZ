---
title: Asynchronous resonance
description:
  How messages, bloodstains and phantoms build empathy between strangers
  who never share a server tick, and why the format has to stay deceivable
tags: [systems, multiplayer]

---

Most multiplayer design assumes contact: a voice channel, a shared instance,
some real-time signal that another player is present. Dark Souls' online
layer refuses all of it and gets a stranger's presence anyway — a message
left on the ground, a bloodstain replaying someone else's death, a
translucent phantom looping their last successful run. None of it is
real-time. All of it still reads as another person, because the format asks
the player to reconstruct intent from a trace rather than receive it live.

The message system is the clearest case of restriction producing meaning.
A fixed vocabulary of templated phrases ("Try", "jump", "ahead") sounds like
a limitation until you notice what it prevents: it can't carry toxicity at
length, and it can't carry certainty either. "Try jumping" left before a
hidden item and "try jumping" left before a fatal drop use identical words.
The genre's own thesis shows up here in miniature — a message is legible
information (a specific claim about a specific spot) that is also,
irreducibly, arbitrary (you cannot verify who left it or why), and the game
never resolves which. Players rate messages up or down, but the vote is a
guess, not a fact-check.

Bloodstains and phantoms extend the same logic into replay. A bloodstain
shows exactly how someone died, which is either a warning or a taunt
depending on tone the format cannot express — the ambiguity is not a bug in
the UI, it's the entire emotional register of "connection in solitude" the
system is built to produce. A cooperative phantom summon adds real-time
contact back in, briefly, which is why it feels different in kind: it's the
one moment the asynchronous system resolves its own ambiguity, and the
genre spends that moment carefully, at fixed points, rather than making it
the default.
