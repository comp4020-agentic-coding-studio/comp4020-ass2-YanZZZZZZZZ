---
title: Level design as argument
description:
  Why a shortcut only earns its name in a map that folds back on itself, and
  how the genre guides a player without a minimap or a quest arrow
tags: [systems, level-design]
related:
  - lectures/week-03
---

A shortcut is meaningless in a level that only ever runs forward — there is
nothing to be short **of**. Dark Souls' geography withholds the player's
own mental map: Firelink Shrine sits at the hub of routes that only reveal
themselves as connected after the player has already walked the long way
around each one, on foot, past the enemies that make the long way cost
something. The elevator that drops you back at the start of Undead Burg is
not a convenience the game hands over; it is the payoff for having survived
the ordeal that justified building it.

This is verticality doing narrative work most level design leaves to
cutscenes. Climbing down from Firelink into the Catacombs, or discovering
that Blighttown's swamp sits beneath the very cliffs you fought across
earlier, teaches the player the shape of the world by making them **feel**
the connections in their legs, so to speak, before they see them on any
map screen. Compare a level that is merely long and winding: length alone
withholds nothing, because a corridor that never reconnects never asks the
player to hold the whole shape in their head at once.

Bloodborne loosens this model — Yharnam is dense and interconnected within
itself, but the game is comfortable with more overtly gated transitions
between its later areas — trading some of the geographic payoff for pacing.
Elden Ring goes further still, opening onto Limgrave's uninterrupted
outdoor space rather than a folded dungeon, which is exactly why its
verticality argument moves from "the whole map connects" to "the whole map
is visible, and the withholding shifts to what you're equipped to survive
in it yet." Both keep the same [legible-failure](/topics/masochism-club/)
standard; the object the geography withholds — connection, or capability —
is what changes.

That standard is also why none of these games ships a minimap or a quest
arrow. A minimap answers "where do I go" by fiat; this genre answers it by
architecture, and the architecture has to do the job with cues that exist
diegetically, in the space itself. Light is the loudest one — a doorway
lit brighter than the corridor around it reads as "forward" before the
player consciously notices why. A bonfire's glow is legible from a
distance for the same reason a streetlight is: it is the one light source
that promises safety, so the eye finds it first. A corpse with a visible
item glow marks a path someone else already survived partway down, which is
a stronger claim than a level designer just placing loot. And a distant,
unreachable landmark — Anor Londo's towers visible from Undead Burg, long
before the game lets you go there — plants a destination in the player's
memory before the route to it exists, so that when the route finally opens,
recognition does the guiding, not a marker.

None of these is a substitute for the map argument above; they're the same
argument at a smaller grain. A shortcut only earns its name because the
player already walked the long way and felt the geography argue something
false about how far apart two places were. Soft guidance only earns its
keep because the player is trusted to actually look — take away the light,
the glow and the landmark, and the level becomes the "merely long and
winding" corridor this topic already dismissed. Withholding a minimap is
not withholding information; it's insisting the information lives in the
space, not in a UI layer floating above it.
