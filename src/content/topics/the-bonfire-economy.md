The bonfire is not a checkpoint. A checkpoint returns you to where you were.
A bonfire returns you to where you were **and** resets every enemy between
there and wherever you die next, **and** takes your accumulated souls with
you as a bet you have to walk back and collect in person. Three separate
decisions — respawn location, enemy state, currency risk — bolted into one
lit fire, so that resting to bank progress and resting to spend it are the
same irreversible action.

That fusion is the bonfire economy, and it is the clearest instance of this
course's thesis: comfort (a place to heal, level up, and travel from) is
never given away for free. It is bought with a specific, named cost — the
run back — and the game is explicit that the cost is the point. Dark Souls
does not hide the run back behind a loading screen or an autosave; it makes
you re-fight it, on the clock, with your souls sitting on the corpse where
you died as a second, visible stake.

Compare the two failure states this produces. Dying with souls unspent and
recovering them is a scare with no lasting cost — the system worked as
insurance. Dying twice before recovering them is the system collecting: the
souls are gone, and the only thing left to show for the run is whatever the
player learned about the stretch of level that killed them twice. The
economy is teaching a specific lesson — cash out your progress before you
gamble it further — and it teaches by consequence, not by a tutorial popup.

Later entries complicate the model without abandoning it. Bloodborne adds
the visceral-attack multiplier, so risk during recovery pays out rather than
merely being avoided; Elden Ring adds Sites of Grace dense enough that the
run back is rarely long, trading tension for traversal speed across a much
larger map. Both keep the core fusion — death costs currency, currency is
recoverable, recovering it is itself risky — which is why the loss still
reads as [legible rather than arbitrary](/topics/masochism-club/) even as
the specific numbers change.
