---
title: Minimal UI and immersion
description:
  Why the genre deletes the quest log and the marker, and what subtraction
  forces a player to remember instead
tags: [systems, ui]
related:

---

A quest log is a promise: whatever you forget, the game will remember for
you. That promise is exactly what a Souls-like withdraws. Dark Souls' entire
HUD is a health bar, a stamina bar, and the item in your hand — no running
objective text, no marker pinned to the next NPC, no journal summarising
what Solaire said about Anor Londo an hour ago. The player's own memory
becomes load-bearing in a way most games spend their UI budget preventing.

This is subtraction as design, not neglect. Every element removed from the
HUD is a decision about what the player now has to hold in their own head:
which key opens which door, what a merchant said about a hollow ally, why a
particular bonfire felt unsafe to rest at. [Level design as
argument](/topics/level-design-and-geography/) already showed how
architecture does guidance work a minimap would otherwise do; minimal UI is
the same subtraction applied to memory rather than navigation — the genre
bets that a fact the player worked to retain lands harder than one a UI
kept re-displaying for them.

The cost of the bet is real, and the genre accepts it openly: players do
forget, do backtrack for a merchant they can't relocate, do die to a boss
mechanic an NPC explained once and never again. That friction is not a
missed feature request. A HUD that restored the quest log would resolve the
friction and remove the reason the world felt worth memorising in the
first place — which is the same trade [the masochism
club](/topics/masochism-club/) test applies to everything else this
course studies: comfort withheld on purpose, not comfort merely absent.
