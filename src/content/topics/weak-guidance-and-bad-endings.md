---
title: Weak guidance and the value of a "bad" ending
description:
  Why ambiguous NPC dialogue functions as navigation, and why a tragic
  ending can outlast a happy one
tags: [narrative, endings]
related:
  - lectures/week-11
---

An NPC who never states their goal plainly is still giving directions — just
not the kind a quest log would print. A figure like a Fire Keeper, tending a
flame and speaking mostly in riddle and half-refusal, tells the player where
significance lives in a level (this room, this flame, this ritual matters)
without ever confirming what to do about it. That's weak guidance's other
face: not just architecture pointing without a marker, but dialogue
pointing without an instruction. The player still has to decide what the
pointing means, which is the whole reason it lands as their discovery
rather than the game's assignment.

Endings extend the same restraint. A genre built on withheld comfort has
little reason to hand back an uncomplicated happy one at the finish line —
and mostly doesn't. Dark Souls' "good" ending rekindles a dying fire for one
more age of a world already established as exhausted by that cycle; its
"bad" ending lets the flame go out and lets something genuinely new begin
in the dark. Calling either one simply good or bad requires ignoring
everything the previous hours argued about what kindling costs and who
pays it.

The craft lesson: an ending's ambiguity has to be earned by the same
restraint the rest of the game practiced, not introduced at the last
minute as a twist. A weak-guidance NPC who suddenly explains everything in
the final cutscene breaks the same contract a HUD that restored the quest
log would break in [minimal UI](/topics/minimal-ui/) — comfort handed back
exactly when the design spent eleven weeks proving it could withhold it on
purpose.
