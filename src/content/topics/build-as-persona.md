title: Build as projection

  Why an attribute allocation is a persona a player projects onto the game,
  and what "no dead points" actually requires of a stat system

Ask a player why they put points into faith instead of strength and the
honest answer is rarely a DPS calculation. It's closer to: this is who I am
in this world. A Souls-like's attribute system is legible enough — few
enough numbers, visible enough thresholds — that the allocation itself
becomes readable as a character choice, the way a tabletop character sheet
is a character choice before it's ever a combat statistic.

That only works if the system genuinely has "no dead points" — every stat
has to buy something a build can actually be built around, not just a
diminishing-returns tax on players who didn't read a wiki. A stat that's
strictly worse than another in every build isn't a persona option, it's a
trap the system pretends is a choice. The test this course keeps returning
to applies here too: a build that fails because the player misjudged a
genuine trade-off is a legible failure; a build that fails because one
attribute was quietly worse in every configuration is
[the arbitrary case](/topics/masochism-club/) wearing a character sheet.

An alternative build — Sekiro's pure-prosthetic-tool run, a faith-only
caster who never swings a melee weapon — is the sharpest test of this,
because it commits to a persona a min-maxed build wouldn't and accepts a
real, nameable cost for it. The pitch for a build like that isn't "this is
secretly optimal"; it's "this is who you'd be if you played this way," and
a system that can support that pitch honestly is doing more work than its
damage-per-second numbers suggest.
